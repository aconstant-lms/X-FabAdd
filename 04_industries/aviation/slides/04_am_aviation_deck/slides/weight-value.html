A kg is a kg

The 3%/h rule holds for any mass carried: passenger, structure or engine. Per aircraft it counts twice: two engines.

New designs: the snowball

1 kg less engine allows 1.5–4 kg less empty weight (pylon, wing, landing gear). Multiply the table by 1.5–4.

The bigger lever: fuel efficiency

A320neo ≈ 2,250 kg/h × 3,500 h ≈ 7,900 t fuel a year. 1% better SFC ≈ 79 t/yr ≈ €50–100k: worth ~750 kg of weight.

Answer to the per-passenger vs per-engine question: the cost-of-weight rule (≈3% per flight hour, Aircraft IT; 3.5%, OpenAirlines; IATA range 2.5–5%) applies to any extra kilogram regardless of where it sits (Scholz, HAW Hamburg: it does not matter whether the kg is operating empty mass or payload). An engine kilogram therefore costs the same fuel to carry as a passenger kilogram, but (1) a twin carries two engines, (2) in a new design each engine kg drives 1.5–4 kg of aircraft empty weight through supporting structure (IPCC Aviation report, ch. 7.4), and (3) for engine parts like the LEAP nozzle the main gain is efficiency, not mass: the LEAP is about 15% more fuel-efficient than the CFM56. A320neo average cruise burn ≈2,250 kg/h (Lufthansa figure via The Flying Engineer). Oct 2026: IATA weekly global average $190.18/bbl (Europe $202.61).
